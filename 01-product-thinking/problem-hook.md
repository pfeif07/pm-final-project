The metric trap: the hidden crisis

SSR counts a plan as resolved the moment it is created, with no inbound contact 30 days before or after. If nothing changes in how the north star is read:

A rising SSR can come from plans that are agreed quickly but never kept.
Missed plans may only return as contact after the 30-day window, so they escape the metric.
Vulnerable or confused consumers who agree just to finish the journey count as success.

The crisis is not only doing nothing. It is optimising a number that can rise while outcomes get worse.

Compounding effects
Cost: Agent time on incomplete, re-checked cases does not fall, even as digital volume grows.
Recovery: Plans that break reduce lifetime returns, which portfolio stakeholders care about more than this month’s cash.
Regulatory and reputational: Pressure to hit a metric without a support path for vulnerable consumers raises fairness and compliance risk.
Scalability: Local fixes built under pressure mix legal rules, client settings and reusable logic, making extension to other markets slower and costlier.
Learning: Without better instrumentation, Riverty can’t tell which plans succeed, so decisions keep rest on guesswork.

- **Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:** Verdict: Systemic, with edge cases layered on top

The moment of misery is a systemic problem. The core pattern (avoidance, fear of judgement, unaffordable plans agreed under pressure) is structural to how staff-led collections work. What varies is its intensity across segments.

Why it’s systemic
Signal	Why it points to structure, not exception
Built into the process	A phone call or letter is the only route to a plan. Anyone who dreads that conversation hits the same wall, regardless of who they are.
Driven by common conditions	Financial stress, shame, and uncertainty about affordability are widespread among people in arrears, not rare.
Visible in behaviour	Googling, spreadsheet budgeting, avoidance, and taking on worse credit are repeated, predictable workarounds.
Shows up in outcomes	Low contact rates, broken plans, and complaints are typical collections metrics, not one-off failures.
Reinforces itself	Silence leads to escalation, which increases dread, which deepens silence.
Where the edge cases sit
Segment	How it differs
Complex or vulnerable cases	Mental health issues, bereavement, or multiple creditors may need a human, not self-service. These are a minority, but important to route correctly.
Disputed debts	The person disagrees that the debt is owed, so an instalment plan is the wrong first step.
Willing and able payers	Some people pay readily and never feel this misery.
Digitally excluded	People without reliable device access or confidence cannot use a self-service channel.
Strategic non-payers	A small group who could pay but choose not to will not respond to a fair plan.
What this means for the product
Design for the common case, protect the edge cases. Self-service should serve the large middle group, with clear detection and hand-off to a human for vulnerability, disputes, or complexity.
Don’t oversell scope. The product is not a universal answer, and claiming so would weaken credibility with regulators and creditors.
Measure the split. The share of cases in each group determines the size of the opportunity.
