# Competitive Analysis & Journey Map (Module 2)

## Responses
- **Role, who are you solving for? (the specific user segment or profile):** A shift-working consumer with two or three open claims and tight finances, trying to sort out repayment on a phone during breaks.
- **Goal, what is this user ultimately trying to achieve?:** Settle all their claims with one affordable plan they understand, without risking more cost or pressure.
- **Friction, the main barrier (moment of misery) stopping them from succeeding:** They hit the income question and can’t tell whether it means this month or a normal month (N-01). They also see only one claim at a time and can’t tell whether paying one stops the letters about the other (N-03, T-202). The fear sits underneath: “What if they use it against me?” (N-06, T-206, since nothing explains why income is asked or how long it is kept). Some give up at that point (“I closed it and meant to come back”). Others push on and pick the lowest-months plan because it looks right, then miss the second payment (N-02). Either way they fall back to the agent, and the portal still logs a started journey.

Supporting evidence: T-201 (853 journeys asked for an income document that never arrived), T-204 (the back button wipes affordability answers), T-207 (reminders sometimes arrive after the due date).
- **External tools, the outside platforms or tools the user is forced to use:** None of your research names an external tool or a manual process. There are no calculators, spreadsheets, screenshots, or notes in N-01 to N-12 or T-201 to T-208. Anything like that would be invented, so I’ve split this into what the evidence shows, what it only implies, and what you’d need to confirm. (I also only have the interview notes and tickets, not a separate Executive Summary, so I’ve used those.)
- **The process, the 3 to 5 manual steps the user takes to get the job done:** 1. Defer and promise to return (N-01)

Opens the portal on a break.
Hits the income question and can’t tell “this month” from “a normal month.”
Closes it, intending to come back.

Why it’s inefficient: A break is the only window a shift worker has, so deferring pushes the task to another scarce window. If they return, T-204 means the back button can wipe their affordability answers, so they may restart. T-207 means a reminder can arrive after the due date. Nothing in the notes shows they do return.

2. Use plan length as a proxy for the right answer (N-02)

Sees several plans without a clear way to test affordability.
Picks the lowest number of months because it “looked like the right answer.”
Finds the instalment too high and misses the second payment.

Why it’s inefficient: The heuristic replaces an affordability check. The shortest plan has the highest instalment, so it is the one most likely to break for someone with tight finances.

3. Call a person or accept the chat’s quick yes (N-04, N-05, N-07)

Gives up on the portal, or wants someone to confirm the cost.
Phones an agent, or takes a fast chat confirmation.
Agrees to a plan, or holds off until someone explains it (N-07).

Why it’s inefficient: The call restarts the journey from zero (N-08: the agent can’t see what was already entered). The chat is fast but doesn’t show what happens after a missed payment (T-208), so the consumer is still unsure (N-05).
- **Core frustration, the exact moment the process feels most “broken”:** It’s a blocking question with no help. T-206 shows there’s no help text explaining why income is asked or how long it’s kept. The consumer can’t resolve the ambiguity inside the portal, so the only moves are to guess or leave.
It lands on the highest-stakes input at the worst time. For someone on a break with tight finances, the answer feels like it determines what they’ll owe, and N-06 shows the added fear that it could be used against them. A wrong guess feels costly, so closing the portal feels safer than answering.
Everything downstream depends on it. The affordability answers, the plan options, and whether the plan holds all hang on that number. If it’s wrong or skipped, you get N-02’s outcome: a plan that looks right and breaks at the second payment.
- **The evidence, a specific quote or behavior from the research that proves this:** N-01 (consumer, two open claims, shift work):

“I opened it on my break. It asked for my income and I did not know if they meant this month or a normal month. I closed it and meant to come back.”

This is the strongest single piece. It contains the full sequence in one person’s words: the context (a break), the trigger (the income question), the ambiguity (this month vs. a normal month), and the outcome (closed it). It is the only item in the research that shows someone leaving the journey at that question.

N-06 (consumer, three open claims, finances tight):

“I was scared to type the wrong number. What if they use it against me?”

This shows the same moment from a second person, with a different cause. N-01 is confusion about what is being asked. N-06 is fear of the consequences of answering. Together they show the question is a problem for two different reasons.
